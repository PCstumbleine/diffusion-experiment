# Fast historical prototype — results (v8)

**What this is:** a quick, real-data sanity check of the diffusion idea, built in one pass rather than through the full ChatGPT review loop. It is deliberately simple and is *not* the rigorous experiment the full spec (`schema.sql`, `edgar_ingest_worker.py`, `statistical_test.py`, etc.) is built to eventually run.

## The test

- **Catalysts:** NVIDIA, AMD, and Broadcom (AVGO) — the three largest suppliers of AI-server silicon (GPUs/accelerators for NVIDIA and AMD, custom AI ASICs and cluster-networking silicon for Broadcom). All three are named, verifiable suppliers to the existing basket: Supermicro's own investor-relations announcements reference both NVIDIA- and AMD-based server products, and Broadcom's networking/ASIC silicon is standard in the same AI-server builds DELL, HPE, and SMCI assemble.
- **Window:** the 16 most recent quarterly earnings releases per company as of August 2026 (each company's own fiscal calendar), locked and primary-source-verified *before* any price reaction data was pulled — full lists in `PREREGISTRATION_v7.md`. NVIDIA via nvidianews.nvidia.com press-release text; AMD via amd.com/ir.amd.com newsroom URLs (which embed the release date); Broadcom via direct SEC EDGAR 8-K filings for CIK 0001730168, using each 8-K's Item 2.02 ("Results of Operations and Financial Condition") to identify the earnings-announcement date — the most rigorous of the three methods.
- **Affected basket:** unchanged since v5 — SMCI, DELL, HPE, equal-weighted, not re-optimized now that there's more data to try against.
- **Benchmarks:** unchanged — SPY and QQQ, matched open→close over the identical entry/exit dates.
- **Strategy:** buy each basket member at the open of the first trading session strictly after each catalyst's earnings release (next-session open, since all three companies report after market close or same-day per their own historical pattern — disclosed as an assumption, not independently re-verified time-of-day for all 48 events), hold 5 trading days, sell. No carried position between events.
- **Return calculation:** adjusted-open → adjusted-close for every instrument, uniformly (the v6 fix, now structural).
- **Unit of inference:** each catalyst event (one company, one quarter) is one observation — up to 48 total, still not fully independent (NVIDIA, AMD, and Broadcom earnings all sit inside the same broad AI-capex supercycle and macro regime, so shared latent-condition risk remains across catalysts, not just within one catalyst's own quarters).
- **Data:** real daily OHLC prices for SMCI, DELL, HPE, SPY, QQQ, NVDA, AMD, and AVGO, pulled from Yahoo Finance, spanning 2022-08-01 to 2026-08-28 (1,024 trading days, identical range, verified for all 8 tickers before any backtest logic ran).

## The critical finding: a real descriptive pattern that formal tests don't confirm

| Catalyst | N | Compounded basket | Compounded SPY (matched) | Compounded QQQ (matched) | Mean excess vs SPY | Wins | Sign-test p (vs SPY) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NVIDIA | 15 | +22.42% | +2.22% | +1.38% | **+1.51%** | 8/15 | **1.000** |
| AMD | 16 | +58.14% | +13.94% | +20.02% | **+2.35%** | 12/16 | **0.077** |
| Broadcom | 16 | −11.95% | −8.01% | −11.00% | **+0.01%** | 10/16 | 0.454 |
| **Pooled** | **47** | **+70.46%** | **+7.14%** | **+8.29%** | **+1.29%** | 30/47 | **0.079** |

Pooled correlation between a catalyst's own overnight earnings-day reaction and the basket's excess return: r = +0.079 (essentially zero; NVIDIA alone is r = −0.028, AMD alone r = −0.133, Broadcom alone r = +0.434 — even this one metric doesn't point the same direction across catalysts).

At a glance, N=47 with mean excess +1.29% and p=0.079 looks like the most "interesting" result this prototype has produced across all eight versions, and the per-catalyst table above looks like it settles the question — AMD's p-value is far smaller than NVIDIA's or Broadcom's. **It doesn't.** A ChatGPT review round caught an important statistical distinction the v7 write-up got wrong: one subgroup having a smaller p-value than another subgroup is not, by itself, evidence that the subgroups' true effects differ. That requires a test of heterogeneity *between* the groups, not three separate tests read side by side. Run directly on this data (`heterogeneity_tests.py`, independently reverified against ChatGPT's own figures to 3–4 decimals):

| Test | Statistic | p-value |
|---|---|---|
| Chi-square (win/loss table) | χ²(2) = 1.593 | 0.451 |
| Exact-conditional (Monte Carlo, 100k perms) | χ² = 1.593 | ≈0.481 |
| One-way ANOVA (excess returns) | F = 0.476 | 0.625 |
| Welch ANOVA (unequal variances) | F = 0.506 | 0.608 |
| Kruskal-Wallis (rank-based) | H = 1.026 | 0.599 |
| Permutation F-test (100k perms) | F = 0.476 | ≈0.629 |

**None of these reject "no difference across catalysts" — every one comes back above p=0.45.** So the honest statement is narrower than v7's: the per-catalyst pattern is real and worth reporting descriptively, but it has not been statistically established as a genuine difference in effects, at this sample size.

That said, the descriptive pattern is not nothing, and a leave-one-catalyst-out check shows how much of the pooled sign-test result depends on it:

| Catalyst excluded | Remaining wins | Sign-test p |
|---|---|---|
| AMD | 18/31 | 0.473 |
| Broadcom | 20/31 | 0.150 |
| NVIDIA | 22/32 | **0.050** |

Removing AMD makes the pooled win-rate pattern largely disappear (p jumps from 0.079 to 0.473); removing NVIDIA actually strengthens it slightly (p=0.050). By share of the pooled *summed* excess return, AMD contributes 62.2%, NVIDIA 37.5%, and Broadcom 0.3% — so AMD is doing most, but not "essentially all," of the work, and NVIDIA's contribution is not negligible even though its win/loss record is exactly at chance (8/15, p=1.000 — a fair description is "no directional win-rate evidence," not "zero relationship," since NVIDIA's mean excess is still +1.51% and the sign test only checks win frequency, not the size or shape of the return distribution).

**Corrected bottom line:** the pooled result should not be read as evidence of a common cross-catalyst diffusion mechanism (that was v7's framing, and it still isn't supported) — but it should equally not be read as confirmed evidence that the catalysts differ from each other, which is what v7's "do NOT behave alike" language implied. The honest state after this round is that **this sample cannot distinguish a common weak effect, a catalyst-specific (plausibly AMD-related) effect, and pure noise.** That's a narrower, more defensible claim than either "there's a unified diffusion effect" or "the catalysts are confirmed to behave differently," and it's the one this version reports.

**One further clarification from a second review round, worth stating explicitly:** failing to establish heterogeneity does not mean the pre-registered pooled result (30/47, p=0.079) is superseded or should be given zero weight. The heterogeneity tests and the pooled test answer different questions — one asks whether NVIDIA/AMD/Broadcom have different effects (not established), the other asks whether the predeclared three-catalyst mixture shows any pattern at all (a weak one, descriptively present). Both can be true at once, and discounting the pooled result *because* AMD looked different afterward would itself be a post-hoc move — letting an observed subgroup pattern retroactively decide whether the pre-registered primary analysis counts. The right role for p=0.079 here is narrow and decision-relevant rather than evidentiary: it's grounds for **continuing to prioritize the prospective Arm A vs. Arm G comparison** (nothing in the expanded historical data discourages that), not grounds for relaxing any evidentiary threshold, promotion gate, or moving toward execution. It changes whether the project keeps testing, not how much evidence is required before anything is traded on.

## Cross-catalyst dependency check

With three catalysts now firing their own 5-day windows independently, a new failure mode became possible that didn't exist in any single-catalyst version: two different catalysts' entry/exit windows could land on overlapping calendar dates, meaning the same underlying basket price action would get counted as "evidence" for two different catalysts at once. This was checked explicitly (all unique cross-catalyst pairs of completed events, tested for date-range intersection — 736 pairs given 15/16/16 events per catalyst: 15×16 + 15×16 + 16×16) rather than assumed away: **result — 0 overlaps**. This is a genuine finding, not a design guarantee, since earnings calendars for three companies with three different fiscal-year structures could plausibly have collided within a 5-trading-day window.

## Honest read

Extending from N=4 (all NVIDIA) to N=47 (three catalysts, 16 quarters each) did increase the raw event count substantially, and the pooled statistic is nominally closer to conventional significance thresholds than any prior version's headline number. The per-catalyst breakdown shows a real descriptive pattern — AMD's own sign-test result is stronger than NVIDIA's or Broadcom's, and AMD contributes most of the pooled summed excess return — but formal heterogeneity testing (chi-square, ANOVA family, Kruskal-Wallis, permutation tests, all above p=0.45) does not establish that this pattern reflects a genuine difference between catalysts rather than sampling noise. So neither "there's a unified cross-catalyst diffusion effect" nor "the catalysts are confirmed to behave differently" is supported by this data. Treating the pooled p=0.079 as "the diffusion effect is getting more real" would be a data-interpretation error; so would treating AMD's smaller p-value as proof that NVIDIA and Broadcom don't matter. The honest conclusion is the same as every prior version, now on a larger and more carefully-tested sample: **interesting example, no credible inference** — with the added, harder-won honest finding that this round's own first attempt at interpreting the heterogeneity overstated what the statistics support, a mistake caught and corrected via the same two-AI verification loop this project has used throughout.

## What's next

Unchanged in priority order from the v6 recommendation (a ChatGPT review round, and this project's own standing rule, both rank the live pipeline above further prototype work):

1. **Primary effort, outside this prototype: run `edgar_ingest_worker.py` continuously against live data and start accumulating real, forward Arm A vs. Arm G outcomes.** No amount of additional historical catalysts, quarters, or statistical tests answers the project's central question (does the LLM beat a cheap mechanical baseline?), because this prototype contains no Arm A/Arm G selection logic, causal graph, or underreaction estimator — it only sanity-checks a much simpler idea. Robinhood/execution integration stays explicitly BLOCKED until a prospective comparison has run and passed the spec's own promotion gate. A ChatGPT review round explicitly reinforced this ranking this round, not just carried it over.
2. **Finish the entry-timing verification this round's pre-registration disclosed as incomplete.** `PREREGISTRATION_v7.md` states the intended rule (verify each event's actual disclosure timestamp) but then discloses that a blanket after-close/same-day assumption was applied to all 48 events instead, for scope reasons — a real, self-disclosed gap between the stated protocol and what was implemented, not a hidden one. Verify all 47 completed events' actual release timestamps against primary sources (NVIDIA's and AMD's own investor-relations pages give exact release times; Broadcom's SEC EDGAR filing-acceptance timestamps do too) and store `release_timestamp`/`timing_source` per event. This is a finite cleanup task, not another research round, and should be finished before this dataset is cited as timing-clean.
3. **If another prototype round happens, treat the AMD-specific pattern as a hypothesis to test prospectively, not a segmentation to search for.** A second ChatGPT review pass sharpened this further and closed a loophole in the first draft of this point: because AMD was selected for follow-up *from these same results* — it was chosen for a closer look precisely because its 12/16 pattern stood out — any split of these same 16 AMD observations remains post-hoc and exploratory even if the breakpoint itself (e.g. its MI300-series ramp) is defined entirely from independent product-history evidence. An externally-defined milestone protects against optimizing the breakpoint (trying many candidate splits and reporting the best one); it does not undo the fact that AMD, rather than NVIDIA or Broadcom, was the subgroup chosen for this treatment because of what these results already showed. The clean version is to freeze a specific AMD/MI300-era hypothesis now, in writing, without running the split against this historical data at all, and test it only against AMD's *future* earnings events — exactly the same logic that makes the live pipeline the priority over further historical search.
4. Add a mechanical, pre-registered rule for flagging (never silently removing) windows with material competing news, covering all three catalysts and all basket members, not just the SMCI-specific prose notes from earlier versions.
5. Keep the 5-day holding window and SMCI/DELL/HPE basket as pre-chosen; don't retroactively re-optimize either now that more catalysts exist to search over.

# The Diffusion Experiment — closing the loop on the original audit

*Written 2026-08-31. This document answers the original 30-question adversarial audit and its A–G deliverables against the current state of the project: the spec series (original design → "The Underreaction Audit" → v2.0 → v2.1 → v2.2 → v2.2.1), the built pipeline code (`schema.sql`, `extraction_prompt_v1.md`, `surprise_transform.py`, `edgar_ingest_worker.py`, `candidate_coverage.py`, `statistical_test.py`), and the fast historical prototype (v1–v5, real data). It does not re-derive the audit from scratch — nearly all of it already has an answer somewhere in that trail. The job here is to say, plainly, what's actually resolved, what's still open, and what to do next.*

## 1. What "the audit" already did

The 30-question document you pasted is, almost word for word, the brief that produced **"The Underreaction Audit"** — the second document in this project's own provenance chain (original design → audit → v2.0 → v2.1 → v2.2 → v2.2.1, as v2.2.1 Section 13 and spec.md Section 12 both state explicitly). In other words: this audit already happened, its findings were merged into v2.0, and three further rounds of adversarial cross-review on top of that produced v2.1 and v2.2.1. So the honest first answer to "please review" is: **most of the 30 questions already have a specific, documented answer in the spec series**, and re-litigating them from zero would just repeat work that already stood up to scrutiny. What follows maps the audit's structure onto what actually resolved it, and — more importantly — flags the handful of things that are still open precisely because nothing before now has tested them against real data.

## 2. The audit's questions, mapped to what closed them

**Economic plausibility, second-order reasoning, market efficiency (Qs 1–4, 12–13):** the core mandate was narrowed rather than defended in the abstract — v2.1 explicitly scopes Phase I to *corporate information diffusion only* (8-Ks, guidance, earnings, supply agreements), defers macro/Fed diffusion to a separate phase, and makes "does an LLM add anything over a cheap mechanical model, given the same candidates" (Arm G) the primary pre-registered comparison, not an afterthought. That's the right structural answer to "is this economically real or an LLM hallucinating causal links" — it doesn't assume the answer, it builds the falsification test in from the start. What it does *not* do is answer the question empirically yet. The fast prototype is the first real attempt, and on the one real test run so far (NVIDIA → a 3-name AI-hardware basket, N=4), the two independent surprise-direction checks — does the size of NVIDIA's beat predict the reaction, does NVIDIA's own market reaction predict it — both show no relationship distinguishable from noise, and one points the "wrong" way. That's not a refutation (N=4 refutes nothing), but it means the plausibility question is still genuinely open, not just theoretically addressed.

**Signal scoring, underreaction measurement, statistical defensibility (Qs 5–8, 14–17):** this is where the review series did the most real work. v2.0's underreaction estimator was static and never updated on intraday price action; v2.1 fixed it to condition on everything observable at decision time. v2.0's Arm G let the LLM both discover *and* rank candidates while the mechanical baseline had no principled way to generate candidates — an apples-to-oranges test; v2.1 forces both through the same candidate universe (`candidate_coverage.py` now checks this holds in the built code). The surprise measure went from an unexplained name to a fully specified `surprise_raw`/`surprise_standardized` pair with an explicit reference source. Multiple-testing correction, pre-registration of one primary hypothesis with a cost-adjusted margin δ, and clustering by catalyst (not raw N) are all specified in v2.1 Section 8. This is a genuinely careful design — more careful than most retail-facing systems of this kind — but it is still a design. `statistical_test.py` exists and has been reviewed three times against live SEC EDGAR data, but it has never yet run against a real accumulated dataset, because there isn't one yet.

**Look-ahead, survivorship, leakage, overfitting (Qs 18–21):** the three-clock bitemporal timestamp taxonomy (`source_published_at` / `source_observed_at` / `canonical_first_public_at`, with explicit precision fields) directly answers the look-ahead question, and was checked against SEC.gov's own documentation of its timestamp precision rather than assumed. Survivorship is handled structurally — permanent `entity_id`s independent of ticker, corporate-actions table — not just promised. The frozen-epoch discipline (train, freeze, score prospectively, never retroactively replace) answers the overfitting question at the design level. All of this is real and was independently checked; none of it has been exercised against a real multi-month dataset yet, which is a different kind of gap than "the design is wrong."

**Sample size, benchmarks, transaction costs, position sizing (Qs 22–25):** the "effective, correlation-adjusted sample size in the low hundreds" gate (v2.1 Section 9) is a genuine, specific answer to "how much data before this means anything" — most designs this size hand-wave that number. The fast prototype's own basket extension this session is a small, direct instance of applying that same discipline: reporting N=4 catalyst events as the number that matters, and explicitly refusing to treat 12 correlated ticker-events as 12 independent ones. Position sizing (fixed-fractional, 5–10% notional per position per v2.2.1 Section 14) and transaction-cost-in-primary-metric are both specified but, again, untested against a real cost model.

**Bull/bear/judge multi-agent structure, correlated LLM error, deterministic safety, extreme events (Qs 26–29):** the seven-arm structure (v2.1's A–G, plus the D/H placebo pair) is a more rigorous answer to "does the multi-agent structure add value" than a bull/bear/judge framing alone — it isolates *which* component (candidate discovery vs. ranking vs. sector beta vs. speed) does any work, rather than just adding LLM opinions and averaging them. The deterministic risk engine and the guardrails in v2.1 Section 11 (insert-only pipeline credential, separated admin access, immutable snapshots, a human-veto role restricted to a fixed disqualifying-conditions checklist) are specified in enough detail to build, and partially built. Extreme-event handling and prompt-injection/cybersecurity treatment (external content is structurally data, never instructions, in every prompt) are stated as day-one guardrails, not retrofits — this is one of the stronger parts of the design.

**Sources, licensing, Robinhood/MCP technical limits, regulatory/tax (Qs 9–11, 30):** WSJ/FT/Economist were deliberately dropped from Phase I specifically because of the licensing question you raised — the design doesn't route around it, it removes the dependency until the question is separately resolved. EDGAR/Fed/IR were adopted as the system of record for exactly the reason you'd expect (free, explicitly machine-licensed, primary-source). The open items list in every version of the spec (most recently v2.1 Section 12) explicitly flags, unresolved: confirming the actual Robinhood account type (cash vs. margin — this changes leverage and PDT treatment materially and was never assumed), re-verifying Robinhood's Trading MCP capabilities and the LLM provider's usage terms close to the actual build date (both had already changed once mid-review), and wash-sale/tax review. These are correctly identified as things nobody has answered yet, not gaps the review missed.

## 3. Deliverables

**(A) Five biggest weaknesses, as they stand today (not as of the original design review — this list is informed by having now run real data through it):**

1. **The core empirical question is still untested at any meaningful scale.** Every structural safeguard in v2.1/v2.2.1 is a hypothesis about how to test the diffusion thesis correctly — none of it is evidence that the thesis is true. The one real test (N=4, three names) found nothing distinguishable from noise on two independent checks.
2. **The fast prototype's own headline number is fragile in exactly the way the spec warns about.** The basket's apparent +26% vs. SPY's +2% is driven almost entirely by one window (May 2026) that also contains a Taiwan export-control investigation and an unrelated cloud-deal announcement — remove that one event and most of the apparent edge disappears. This is the "no single trade/category driving the result" promotion gate failing, on the very first real test.
3. **Nothing about Robinhood integration itself has been built or tested.** The entire spec series treats Robinhood as a Phase II/III execution target, gated behind statistical evidence that doesn't exist yet. There is no code, and there should not yet be code, that talks to the Robinhood Trading MCP.
4. **The real ingestion pipeline has been code-reviewed but never run continuously against live data.** `edgar_ingest_worker.py` has survived three rounds of review against live SEC.gov data, but "survives review" and "has produced a real, growing `candidate_signals` table over weeks of live operation" are different claims, and only the second one lets the statistical framework in Section 8 actually do anything.
5. **The retail-edge question — arguably the single most important open question in the whole project — has not been tested even once.** Arm A vs. Arm G (does the LLM add anything over a cheap mechanical baseline) is the entire point of the seven-arm design, and it has never been run.

**(B) Five strongest aspects (this hasn't changed, and deserves saying plainly — this is a genuinely careful design):**

1. The three-clock bitemporal timestamp model, checked against SEC.gov's own stated timestamp precision rather than assumed.
2. Arm G as a mandatory, pre-registered primary comparison — the design refuses to let itself claim credit for the LLM without a cheap baseline to beat.
3. The two-probability, three-axis evidence model (source authority / relationship evidence / shock-transmission evidence, tracked separately) — this is a more careful treatment of "is this causal link real" than most systems in this space attempt.
4. The promotion-gate structure itself: numeric, pre-specified thresholds at every phase transition, including "no single trade/category driving the result" — which, per weakness #2 above, is already earning its keep.
5. The willingness, at every stage of this review, to write down what's still unverified rather than paper over it (the Robinhood account-type question, the licensing question, the wash-sale question) — the open-items sections are honest, not decorative.

**(C) Five most important changes — nearly all already made; here's what's left:**

1. *(Done, v2.1)* Fix the underreaction estimator to update on intraday price action.
2. *(Done, v2.1)* Force Arm A and Arm G through the same candidate set.
3. *(Done, this session)* Stop relying on a single affected ticker — the basket extension (SMCI/DELL/HPE) is exactly this change, applied to the prototype.
4. *(Not yet done)* Add a **mechanical, pre-registered rule** for censoring or flagging catalyst windows with material unrelated competing news, rather than handling it with prose notes after the fact. The May 2026 window in the prototype shows exactly why this can't stay ad hoc — a discretionary "I'll flag it if I notice it" review process is itself a source of bias.
5. *(Not yet done)* Actually run `edgar_ingest_worker.py` continuously against live data for long enough to accumulate real `candidate_signals` rows — everything downstream of this (Arm A vs. Arm G, the statistical framework, any promotion decision) is blocked on this existing.

**(D) Revised architecture:** this is `diffusion-experiment-spec-v2.2.1.md`, already written and already the terminal document of the conceptual-design phase (its own Section 13 says so). Nothing in this review changes that architecture — it holds up.

**(E) Minimum viable prototype:** this is the combination of the built pipeline code (`schema.sql`, `extraction_prompt_v1.md`, `surprise_transform.py`, `edgar_ingest_worker.py`, `candidate_coverage.py`, `statistical_test.py`) and the fast historical prototype (now v5, attached). The pipeline code implements the real MVP; the prototype is a real-data sanity check that the underlying idea is even worth building the MVP for — and its honest answer, four events in, is "not yet demonstrated, not yet ruled out."

**(F) A 30-day testing plan (this did not exist yet — written now):**

- **Days 1–5:** Deploy `edgar_ingest_worker.py` as a continuously running worker against live SEC EDGAR RSS/XBRL and the Fed release calendar (Phase I-A scope: corporate events only). Confirm it produces well-formed `extracted_events` and `candidate_signals` rows, including rejections, on live traffic — this is the Section 9 "Build → running Phase I-A" gate, and it has not been checked yet.
- **Days 1–30 (parallel):** Extend the fast prototype's historical window per its own "what's next" item — the previous 12–16 NVIDIA quarters, researched and verified before looking at any results, not searched backward until something interesting appears. This is the cheapest lever available to increase effective N beyond 4, and doesn't require the live pipeline to be running.
- **Days 5–20:** Once live candidates are flowing, hand-label a held-out sample for the pipeline-quality metrics in Section 9 (citation-validity precision/recall, entity-resolution accuracy, relationship-link precision/recall, duplicate-event precision/recall) — distinct from whatever sample would later tune the extraction prompt.
- **Days 20–30:** Run Arm A vs. Arm G on whatever live candidates have accumulated, purely as a dry run of the statistical machinery (`statistical_test.py`) — not as a promotion decision, since 30 days won't reach the "low hundreds, correlation-adjusted" sample size gate. The goal is to confirm the pipeline end-to-end (extraction → candidates → arms → statistical test) runs correctly on real data, not to get an answer yet.
- **Ongoing:** Do not touch Robinhood, MCP, or any execution code during this window. There is nothing in this 30-day plan that requires it, and building it now would be building ahead of the evidence the whole design says to wait for.

**(G) Recommendation: PROCEED — WITH MAJOR CHANGES, and explicitly not yet toward Robinhood.**

The conceptual design (v2.2.1) is sound enough to keep building on without another review round — three rounds of adversarial cross-review, each checked against primary sources, is more scrutiny than most systems like this get before a dollar is at risk. But "the design is sound" and "the thesis is true" are different claims, and right now only the first one has support. The single most important thing this review surfaces is that **the project has, without anyone deciding it explicitly, drifted toward asking "how do we connect this to Robinhood" before asking "does Arm A ever beat Arm G."** That ordering should reverse. The major change isn't to the architecture — it's to what gets built next: the live ingestion pipeline and the Arm A vs. Arm G comparison, not Robinhood/MCP integration, which stays exactly where the spec's own promotion gates already put it (Phase II, gated behind statistical evidence that doesn't exist yet).

## 4. Scores (0–100), as the evidence stands today

- **Economic plausibility: 45.** The mechanism is coherent and the corporate-diffusion scoping is a real improvement over a vague "information takes time to price in" claim — but the one real test found nothing, and N=4 can't move this number much in either direction.
- **Technical feasibility: 70.** Every individual component has been built and independently verified against live data at least once. The gap is integration at scale and over time, not any single piece being broken.
- **Statistical rigor: 80 for the design, ~10 for evidence gathered.** The pre-registration, clustering, multiple-testing, and frozen-epoch framework is genuinely rigorous. It has essentially nothing to operate on yet.
- **Risk control: 75.** Deterministic gates, phased rollout, a human-veto role restricted to a fixed checklist, and guardrails specified from day one — all real, none yet exercised under live conditions.
- **Retail edge: 25.** This is the least-supported number in the whole review, deliberately. Arm A vs. Arm G is the entire test of whether a retail participant without a licensed real-time feed or colocated execution can realistically compete on this kind of diffusion, and it has never been run.
- **Scalability: 40.** The architecture (versioned configs, append-only tables, restricted DB roles) supports scaling in principle. Every number above it is what actually gates whether scaling is warranted.

## 5. What changed this session

The fast prototype was extended from a single affected ticker (SMCI) to a predeclared three-name basket (SMCI/DELL/HPE), and QQQ was added as a second benchmark alongside SPY — the two items from the v4 "what's next" list that were achievable with data already in hand. The result (attached: `diffusion_prototype_v5.zip`, chart, and updated `PROTOTYPE_README.md`) is more informative than a "yes it works" or "no it doesn't": the basket-level headline number looks much stronger than the single-ticker one, but it is still dominated by a single, multiply-confounded event, which is precisely the failure mode the project's own promotion gates exist to catch — and it caught it, on the very first real test, at essentially no cost. That's the whole design working as intended, even though the underlying question remains open.

The one basket/window item *not* done this session — extending the historical window to 12–16 NVIDIA quarters — is flagged rather than rushed, because doing it honestly means researching and independently verifying additional historical earnings dates before looking at any results, which deserves its own pass.
